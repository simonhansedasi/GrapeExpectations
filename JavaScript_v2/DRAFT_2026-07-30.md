# Bounded ground: terrain limits invert climate-only suitability projections

## Abstract

Climate suitability projections for perennial crops typically map a species' climatic envelope onto a landscape and report where that envelope will sit under warming. We show that this procedure can invert the sign of the result when the crop occupies terrain that is bounded in the direction the envelope migrates. Writing the feasible set as `F(dT) = C(dT) ∩ S` — a migrating climatic window intersected with a stationary feasibility set defined by terrain — the climate-only and intersected projections disagree in sign once the window's displacement along the migration axis exceeds the headroom between its leading edge and `S`'s upper bound. For Hawaiian coffee that threshold is already crossed: a +1.35 °C displacement moves the thermal window 236 m upslope against 197–224 m of available headroom. The consequence is measurable on a matched statistic. Across the same displacement the thermal set is effectively unchanged in size (−0.3%), while the feasible set contracts by 22.1%. The contraction is therefore attributable to the terrain screen rather than to the climatic window, and the direction of the result reverses rather than merely its magnitude. The contraction survives every discretionary parameter tested. We treat the island's two designated coffee origins as one unit, because no contrast between them is resolvable at this sample size.

## 1. Introduction

The standard move in crop climate-suitability work is to fit a climatic envelope from occurrence records, project it under a warming scenario, and map where the envelope lands. The output is a map of gains and losses. The implicit assumption is that the crop can follow its envelope: that if suitable climate appears at 1,200 m, the crop can be grown at 1,200 m.

For perennial crops on constrained terrain that assumption fails, and it fails in a specific and consequential way. Coffee is planted on ground that satisfies conditions having nothing to do with climate — workable slope, sufficient soil depth, access, absence of recent lava. That ground does not move, and on a shield volcano it does not extend indefinitely upslope. The climatic window does move. The land actually available to the crop is the intersection, and the behaviour of an intersection between a moving set and a bounded stationary one is not the behaviour of either.

We formalise this as `F(dT) = C(dT) ∩ S`, where `C(dT)` is the set of cells whose temperature falls within the crop's thermal band after a shift `dT`, and `S` is the terrain footprint. Two results follow. The first is a stated condition under which a climate-only projection and an intersected one disagree in sign — not merely in magnitude — which is what makes the finding transferable to systems other than this one. The second is that Hawaiian coffee satisfies that condition now, on measured quantities rather than assumed ones.

Hawaiian coffee is a good demonstration case rather than the subject. A designation of origin makes `S` explicit and mappable: the industry publishes where its farms are, so the feasibility set is observed rather than modelled.

## 2. Data

**Study system.** 471 labelled coffee cells on the Island of Hawai'i at 500 m resolution — 409 in the Kona district, 62 in Ka'u — derived from 674 coffee polygons in the 2020 Hawai'i Agricultural Land Use Baseline. The island contributes 42,524 land cells, of which 36,055 are farmable (workable slope, not on historic lava).

**Temperature.** A 250 m gridded climatology (Kodama et al. 2024) covering 1990–2021, obtained as 384 monthly rasters. Suitability is evaluated on a lapse-rate surface, and thermal envelopes are fit on that same surface — an earlier version fit envelopes on one temperature product and evaluated suitability on another.

**Rainfall.** Annual means from the Rainfall Atlas of Hawai'i (Giambelluca et al. 2013, 250 m, 1978–2007); monthly climatology from 384 HCDP rasters (1990–2021). Both replace a ~4 km product used in earlier versions of this analysis which was found to have the two districts **inverted**, recording Kona at 6,471 mm/yr against Ka'u at 741 when the correct values are 1,321 and 1,783. Two independent products now agree that Ka'u is the wetter district, by ratios of 1.35 (Atlas) and 1.26 (HCDP monthly sums).

**Terrain and soil.** Slope, aspect, local and total relief, and distance to coast from the USGS DEM; soil properties from gSSURGO. Elevation enters the feature set once. A previous version included both `elev_mean` and `elev_dev_mean`, which is `elev_mean` minus a scalar and correlates with it at r = 1.000, double-weighting elevation on precisely the axis along which the thermal band migrates.

**Warming increments, and what they are anchored to.** This needs stating precisely because every result below is a statement about a displacement, and a displacement is meaningless without an origin. `dT` is anomaly from the 1990–2021 observational climatology, whose midpoint is **2006** — not from the present. The values +1.00 and +1.35 °C used throughout are **round reference increments, not ensemble projections**. The NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 ensemble deltas are +0.60 °C (2035) and +0.92 °C (2045) under SSP2-4.5, and +1.41 and +1.78 °C under SSP5-8.5, each differenced against that model's 2001–2014 historical window. The reference increments lie inside that range and carry no scenario interpretation. The two baseline periods differ (2001–2014 for the GCM window against 1990–2021 for the observational field), but their midpoints are 2008 and 2006 — two years apart, which at the belt trend below is **0.063 °C** and is negligible against every quantity reported here. What is not negligible is the gap to the present: as §4.2 shows, roughly +0.49 °C of any displacement quoted against the climatological origin has already been realised, so +1.35 °C corresponds to about **+0.86 °C of further warming from the end of the observational record**.

## 3. Methods

**The feasibility set `S`.** Farm-cell features are standardised, a centroid computed per district, and `S` defined as cells within the 95th percentile of farm-cell distance to the nearer centroid. Every component — standardiser, both centroids, and the percentile cutoff — is refit inside each bootstrap replicate, so intervals do not inherit the point estimate's threshold. `S` covers 15.2% of farmable island land.

**The climatic set `C(dT)`.** A Gaussian thermal envelope per district, fit on farm-cell temperatures with a 1.5x variance inflation. `C(dT)` is the `tau = 0.5` level set, spanning `mu ± sqrt(2 ln 2) · sigma`.

**Uncertainty.** Spatial block bootstrap, with blocks formed by k-means on farm-cell coordinates. Because block size is discretionary and sets every interval width in the analysis, we report results across block sizes from 4 to 24 cells per block.

**Analysis unit.** The pooled union `F_kona ∪ F_kau`. Section 4.1 gives the empirical justification.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 The two districts are not distinguishable, and are treated as one

Kona and Ka'u are marketed as distinct origins, and the natural question is whether they face different futures. The data cannot support the question being asked.

Their thermal envelopes are 0.18 °C apart (Kona `mu` = 21.00 °C, Ka'u 20.82 °C). Their seasonal temperature amplitudes are identical (3.23 and 3.24 °C). Every contrast we can construct spans zero: the difference in feasible-set decline to 2045 is −8.2 pp with a 95% interval of (−29.2, +12.3), and that verdict is unchanged at every spatial block size tested. Cup scores from five years of statewide competition data (284 lots, 91 farms) show no district difference once variety and processing are controlled: +0.02 points, 95% CI (−0.46, +0.57).

The limitation is structural rather than a matter of precision. Ka'u contributes 62 cells, and district classification from environmental variables is circular in any case — the districts are geographically disjoint, so no spatially honest cross-validation exists. Partitioning the farm cells into 6, 12, or 24 spatial blocks yields **no block containing both districts**. We therefore report the pooled union throughout.

**One axis does separate them, and it is not the one usually assumed.** Annual rainfall overlaps by 475 mm. But the monthly cycles are anti-correlated (r = −0.58): Kona peaks in September, Ka'u in March (Figure `02_rainfall_phase`). Temperature peaks in August in both, so Kona's rain arrives with the heat (corr(T, R) = +0.90) and Ka'u's arrives against it (−0.46). Expressed as the temperature at which rain falls minus the annual mean, Kona sits at +0.30 °C and Ka'u at −0.15 °C (Figure `02_separation`).

The separation on that derived field is clean, and we deliberately do not lean on how clean. Two spatially disjoint regions will separate on almost any smoothly varying derived quantity, which is the same fact that denies us an honest cross-validation above; a 0.058 °C gap between the districts' ranges is not independent evidence of a distinction. We report the phase contrast as a characterisation, not a mechanism, and one predicted consequence has already failed. A five-month rainfall offset should shift the crop calendar; tested against documented harvest windows it does not, with both districts harvesting from late summer to midwinter and peak picking in September–December. Neither district has a genuine dry season (driest months 52 and 55 mm), so the rainfall-triggered flowering that produces calendar offsets elsewhere does not operate here.

### 4.2 "Present climate" is not the present

The temperature field is a 1990–2021 climatology with midpoint 2006. Regressing its own belt-mean temperature on year across 32 complete years gives a trend of **+0.313 ± 0.065 °C/decade**, placing the end of the observational record **+0.486 ± 0.100 °C** above the climatological mean (Figure `03_baseline_envelope`). Offsets measured against the climatological zero must be re-centred before they can be read as statements about now, and §2 gives the resulting correction for the reference increments.

For scale, the seasonal temperature cycle in the coffee belt is 3.23 °C, so the +1.35 °C reference increment is 42% of the entire annual swing, imposed permanently.

### 4.3 The inversion, on a matched statistic

The comparison has to be made on the same statistic computed twice, once with the screen off and once with it on. Comparing a directional gradient measure over all farmable land against a set-cardinality change over the footprint would not establish that `S` is doing the work, because a cell can move closer to the thermal optimum and still lie outside `C`.

Computing `|C(dT)|/|C(0)|` and `|C(dT) ∩ S|/|C(0) ∩ S|` across the reference increments:

```
unit      dT       |C|     dC%       |F|     dF%
kona    +1.00     9,417    +0.3     1,349   -18.3
kona    +1.35     9,365    -0.3     1,215   -26.4
kau     +1.00     7,134    +1.7     1,660    -9.6
kau     +1.35     7,174    +2.2     1,502   -18.2
pooled  +1.00     9,417    +0.3     3,009   -13.7
pooled  +1.35     9,365    -0.3     2,717   -22.1
```

**The thermal set alone is flat.** Across a displacement that removes a fifth of the feasible set, `|C|` changes by −0.3% pooled, and by +2.2% for Ka'u — if anything it grows. The entire contraction is attributable to the intersection with `S`. This is a stronger and narrower claim than a comparison between "most of the island gains" and "the feasible set shrinks", and unlike that comparison it isolates the screen as the cause (Figure `04_inversion`).

The direction of the error therefore reverses rather than merely shrinking. A climate-only reading of this landscape reports a thermal resource that is stable to slightly improving; the feasible resource is contracting at 22.1% over the same interval.

### 4.4 The condition under which the screen flips the sign

Both `|C|` and `|F|` change by the same accounting: cells recruited at the window's cool leading edge, minus cells lost at its warm trailing edge. They disagree in sign when the leading edge still recruits farmable land but no longer recruits feasible land. This happens once the window's displacement along the migration axis exceeds the headroom between its leading edge and `S`'s upper bound.

With `Gamma` the lapse rate, displacement is `dz = dT / Gamma`, and the sufficient condition for inversion is `dz > h`, with `h` the headroom. On the coffee belt `Gamma` = 5.73 °C/km, so +1.35 °C displaces the window **236 m** upslope. Measured headroom is **197 m** for Kona and **224 m** for Ka'u, giving `dz/h` of **1.19** and **1.05**. Both exceed one, which is the quantitative content of the inversion.

The edge accounting shows it directly. For Kona at +1.35 °C, 260 farmable cells enter at the leading edge against 303 leaving — near balance — while inside `S` only 16 enter against 56 leaving. The share of `S` still lying above the window's leading edge falls from 17.6% at the climatological baseline to 3.7% at +1.00 °C and **1.0%** at +1.35 °C; for Ka'u, from 23.4% to 2.1%. The window is running out of feasible ground to move into while farmable ground remains abundant above it.

This is the transferable part. A reader in another system can evaluate `dz/h` from a lapse rate, a projected displacement, and the upper bound of their own feasibility set, without reproducing anything else here.

### 4.5 What is geometry and what is terrain

Sweeping `dT` from −3 to +3 °C, `|F|` rises to a maximum and falls away on both sides (Figure `04_F_vs_dT`), with peaks at −0.65 °C (Kona) and +0.15 °C (Ka'u) against the climatological mean; re-centred on the end of record, **−1.14 °C** and **−0.34 °C**.

We do not present the location of that peak as a finding, and it should not be read as one. `C` is fit on farm-cell temperatures and `S` is a shell around farm-cell feature centroids. Both are centred on the same 471 cells, so an intersection peaking near `dT = 0` is a property of the construction. Defining the optimum as where the crop is and then reporting that the crop sits near its optimum is not a result.

What is empirical is that `S` is **bounded in the direction the window migrates, and bounded closely**. The same construction with an `S` extending further upslope would yield a plateau or a rightward peak; it is the 197–224 m ceiling of §4.4, against a 236 m displacement, that produces a descending limb instead. Consistent with this, `|C|` alone peaks at −1.15 °C (Kona) and −1.75 °C (Ka'u) — nowhere near `dT = 0` — so the intersection's peak position is not inherited from the climatic set.

The defensible statement is about boundedness rather than optimality: the feasible set has no upslope pathway to expand into, so no warming increment increases it, and the asymmetry is a terrain fact rather than a modelling artefact.

### 4.6 Robustness

The pooled union declines **13.7%** by 2035 and **22.1%** by 2045.

The magnitude depends on how heavily elevation is weighted in the screen, running from −12.0% at `w_z = 0` to −37.5% at `w_z = 2`. The interval excludes zero at every weight, including `w_z = 0` (Figure `05_robustness`). That test is worth stating carefully. Setting `w_z = 0` removes elevation's *label* from the screen, not elevation's *information*: on a shield volcano, slope, local and total relief, distance to coast and most soil properties all covary strongly with elevation. The honest reading is that elevation is not separable from the remaining terrain descriptors on this landscape, so the test **constrains** the shared-coordinate objection without settling it. It bounds how much of the contraction could be an artefact of `C` and `S` sharing a coordinate; it does not demonstrate that none of it is.

The verdict is likewise unchanged across spatial block sizes from 4 to 24 cells per block.

The magnitude should be quoted as a range across screen weightings, not as a point estimate. The sign and the significance are robust; the number is not a measurement.

## 5. Discussion

The methodological claim is the transferable one, and §4.4 states it as a condition rather than an anecdote. Any suitability projection for a crop confined to particular ground is exposed to sign inversion when the projected displacement along the migration axis exceeds the feasibility set's headroom in that direction. The exposure is largest exactly where such analyses are most often applied — mountainous terrain, where climatic gradients are steep, so a given warming buys a large displacement, and where the land satisfying the new climate is frequently land that satisfies nothing else. The correction is not exotic: it requires only that the feasibility set be defined, bounded, and intersected. But omitting it does not produce a conservative error. It produces the opposite sign.

The second claim is about interpretation. A feasible set that is bounded in the direction of migration has no upside pathway, whatever the climate does. The relevant question is not whether conditions will improve — under this geometry they cannot — but how quickly they degrade and what the crop's tolerance to displacement is.

For Hawaiian coffee specifically, the finding is that the island's usable coffee ground has effectively exhausted its upslope headroom, and that the two designated origins face this as one region rather than two. That contradicts the framing under which the districts are marketed, and it is worth stating plainly that we found no evidence of the environmental distinction that framing implies — in thermal niche, in feasible-set trajectory, or in the cup.

## 6. Limitations

**Ka'u is underpowered.** 62 cells in a spatially contiguous district. No district-level estimate should be read as comparable to Kona's, and we do not report any as such.

**The screen is weakest where the argument lives.** Elevation-band cross-validation recovers the upper fringe of the belt less well than its interior — and the upper fringe is exactly where the headroom of §4.4 is measured. Specific upper-fringe cells cannot be nominated with uniform confidence, and `h` inherits that uncertainty.

**The terrain break is diffuse.** Terrain above the footprint differs sharply in kind, but a free-breakpoint fit places the transition anywhere from 660 to 1,330 m depending on descriptor. We do not claim a sharp boundary.

**Elevation is not separable.** See §4.6. The `w_z = 0` test bounds the shared-coordinate concern rather than eliminating it.

**The magnitude is screen-dependent.** See §4.6.

**Cup evidence is truncated.** Competition results publish only scores of 80 and above, so the null district difference applies to competition-grade lots, not to median product. Score reliability also caps any site model: the intraclass correlation of farm identity is 0.26, so three-quarters of score variance is lot-to-lot.

**No management covariate.** A parallel attempt to relate canopy greenness (NDVI) to environment returned negative cross-validated `R²` for both districts, on corrected and original data alike. We interpret this as management — pruning cycle, block age, shade — dominating site, and report no canopy result.

---

## 7. Conclusions

The land available to a perennial crop is the intersection of a moving climatic window and stationary feasible ground. When that ground is bounded in the direction of migration, and the projected displacement exceeds the remaining headroom, a climate-only projection and an intersected one disagree in sign rather than in magnitude. Hawaiian coffee has crossed that threshold: 236 m of displacement against 197–224 m of headroom. Over the same interval the thermal set is unchanged in size to within a third of a percent, while the feasible set contracts by 22.1%.
